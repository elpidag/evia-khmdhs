For those of us not experiencing them firsthand, wildfires increasingly unfold through a continuous stream of images circulating across television broadcasts, news websites, social-media platforms, live feeds and mobile-phone screens.[FIGURE 01: Images of fires] Over the past two decades, “a planet on fire” has become a familiar refrain during longer and more intense fire seasons. Images of people trying to save their homes, fleeing affected areas, or watching their livelihoods turn to ash remain central to media coverage, even as drone footage and satellite imagery increasingly offer a distant, “God’s-eye view” of landscapes on fire or already burnt.

Greece is part of the wider Mediterranean Basin, where exposure to extreme fire-weather conditions has increased over recent decades as a result of climate change<sup>1</sup>. In Greece, the numerous, extensive and frequent fires of recent years have attracted sustained national and international attention throughout the summer months. This burning interest, along with emotional debates about the fires’ impact, heated discussions about the fire brigade's insufficient resources, inadequate state measures for fire prevention and possible causes of ignition and news reports about possible suspects of arson, usually blows over as soon as August ends and the majority of us abandon the countryside and the periphery yet again, fleeing towards Athens and other city centres to resume our hectic urban lifestyles.  Once the fire season subsides, reporting becomes more fragmented, shifting towards post-fire restoration, emergency state measures, community support, and corporate relief initiatives. What receives far less sustained scrutiny are the legislative and policy changes introduced in the aftermath of major fires, and the ways these changes reshape land use, land management and, increasingly, forest governance. 

The importance of this omission becomes clearer if we acknowledge that fire regimes<sup>2</sup> are shaped not only by climatic conditions but also by transformations in how land is used, managed and regulated. The environmental, social and economic effects of fires therefore cannot be disconnected from their political effects. This investigative report, presented through this online platform, treats large fires as “agents”, insofar as they become consequential events through which policy change can be justified, accelerated or reframed. In this sense, large fires do not only abruptly and drastically transform the natural landscapes they sweep through; they can also contribute to transforming the legal and administrative frameworks through which those landscapes are governed. Measures introduced in response to one fire season may, in turn, influence land management, prevention practices and forest governance in the seasons that follow. It is through this lens that this investigative report asks two related questions: what can be gained by monitoring policy and legislative changes in the aftermath of fires, and how can making administrative registries legible help trace changes in land management practices and their implications for fire regimes? The report examines these questions through the case of Greece, focusing on the period from 2018 onwards.
