[FIGURE 02: Fires in 2007]The fires that swept southern Greece in the summer of 2007, particularly the Peloponnese and Evia, burned vast areas of forest and agricultural land and caused 85 deaths. The fires of 2007 stand out as a major extreme-fire year in Greece’s recent wildfire history, marked by an exceptional concentration of large and destructive fires. Recent analyses place them within an emerging shift towards increasing fire severity and the recurrence of very large fires<sup>3</sup>.

[FIGURE 03: Fires in 2018] Their deadly toll was surpassed in 2018 by the fire in Mati, in eastern Attica, where 104 people lost their lives. In the aftermath of this fire, among the main points of heated public debate, was the lack of organised procedures for early evacuation processes of the settlements in the proximity of the fire. During this period of highly politicised debates regarding responsibility for the impact of the fire, the newly elected government of New Democracy inaugurated the pilot use of the outbound, alerting component of the 112 Emergency Communications Service in August 2019<sup>4</sup>. During the COVID-19 lockdowns that followed, the 112 emergency alerts were used as a means of state communication, but it was during the fire season of 2021 their deployment was intensified even more.

[FIGURE 04: 112 emergency alerts] The 2021 fire season was the first one during which 112 emergency alerts were used for informing citizens regarding fires in their proximity and for giving direct instructions of evacuations in settlements close to ongoing fires. During the fires of that season, 125,000 hectares of forest and arable land were burnt, with over 50,000 hectares burnt in North Evia alone. According to residents of the fire-affected settlements of North Evia — Agia Anna, Kechries and Limni — this amplified use of the alerts shaped the public's response, especially during the first of the ten consecutive days during which the fire swept the northern part of the island. Vaggelis, a resin collector from Kechries; Michalis, a carpenter from Agia Anna; Nikos, a volunteer from outside the area; and George, a forest worker and farmer from Kamatriades, all pointed in the same direction in our conversations: not only were the firefighting forces sent to the area very limited, there seemed to be no organised plan of intervention to put out the fire, and no coordinated plan for the evacuation itself. Although the messages on their phones, and the police urging people to leave, named destinations, in many cases the evacuation route meant driving through areas that were already burning.

While the fires in North Evia were still ongoing, this state of emergency was being translated into ‘an opportunity’ for the future development of the region. [FIGURE 05: Press conference] During a press conference the Prime Minister announced a targeted reconstruction programme for North Evia, orally assigning its development to the NGO Diazoma and its president Stavros Benos along with a key legislative change concerning the incorporation of the private sector in post fire restoration.

For the reconstruction programme in North Evia, a Special Committee was formed chaired by Mr. Benos, composed partly of Diazoma members, and supplemented by scientists and consultants selected by Mr. Benos himself. Through the vision included in the Masterplan study and its 13 accompanying studies, each funded by an individual sponsor, the programme set out provisions for infrastructure, environmental restoration, agriculture and the local food economy, education and employment, health and welfare, tourism and cultural heritage — directives, in effect, for reorganising the area's production model, its character, and its future marketing as a tourist destination. As the Chamber for the Environment and Sustainability put it before the Hellenic Parliament's Special Permanent Committee on Environmental Protection, the programme had the characteristics of a new spatial plan for North Evia, extending beyond the general principles of spatial and regional planning into urban planning itself. What was presented as a programme for post-fire recovery developed into a comprehensive spatial and urban-planning project, in which the language of urgency legitimised speed, exceptional procedures and the mobilisation of private-sector actors, even where the programme's content exceeded the logic of emergency response.

After the completion of the studies, most of the projects they proposed, were incorporated into the Integrated Territorial Investment<sup>5</sup> scheme for North Evia. [FIGURE 06: Special Urban Plan -land use maps] Importantly, two of the fourteen donated studies were Special Urban Plans<sup>6</sup> for the municipalities of Mantoudi–Limni–Agia Anna and Istiaia–Aidipsos. The fire affected both municipalities but neither in its entirety; the plans were nevertheless drafted for the full territorial extent of both. The plan for Mantoudi–Limni–Agia Anna was presented by the team responsible for its design with a dual mandate: to address the aftermath of the wildfire and mitigate subsequent risks such as floods, and to complete first-level urban planning across the whole municipality, citing the lack or inadequacy of existing planning<sup>7</sup>. Yet by the time of the fires, the municipal unit of Limni already had a General Urban Plan approved in 2020, and those of Kireas and Nileas had plans in the final stages of authorisation. Special Urban Plans carry greater regulatory weight and can override the provisions of General Urban Plans; even ones that, as here, had gone through years of revision, conflict mediation and negotiation with local stakeholders.

Nearly five years after the fires, neither of these two plans is in effect. In the case of the municipality of Istiaia–Aidipsos, while the donated plan was submitted to the Ministry of Environment and the competent authorities for review after Phase A of its design was completed, a Local Urban Plan covering the same boundaries was initiated, under the Technical Chamber's "Konstantinos Doxiadis" programme. The latter was the one that is the one that is currently in Phase A of the main study, having advanced to the evaluation and consultation stage, while the donated Special Urban Plan was not put into use<sup>8</sup>. In the case of the Special Urban Plan for the municipality of Mantoudi–Limni–Agia Anna, although the municipal council formally accepted the final study in July 2025 (decision 126/2025), in early 2026, residents of the area applied to the Council of State for the annulment of that acceptance, contesting the legality of the Plan being assigned to, and prepared by, non-state bodies.

The residents’ recourse to the Council of State raises the same question posed by Maria Karamanof, president of the Chamber for the Environment and Sustainability: on what legal basis was the planning of a region assigned to a private committee? Under the Greek Constitution, spatial planning falls under state authority. Private expertise may be used, but through legally prescribed tendering procedures intended to safeguard transparency and public accountability. Karamanof also stresses that a donated spatial plan cannot be understood as a neutral gift as it already contains the substance of a future administrative decision<sup>9</sup>. The issue, therefore, is not private participation alone, but the displacement of public authority and procedure, raising questions of legitimacy, environmental protection and the public interest.

The scheme of the restoration and reforestation contractor, announced by the Prime Minister on 12 August 2021 alongside the reconstruction programme for North Evia, was enacted through an emergency legislative act the following day and, less than three weeks later, ratified by Parliament through Law 4824/2021. [FIGURE 07: reforestation sponsor image-media] The new provision added to the general legislation on forest management and protection (Law 998/1979) allows any organisation, public or private, whether for-profit or non-profit, to be designated as a contractor for the restoration and reforestation of burnt public forests and forested areas that have been declared reforestable, or that meet the conditions to be so declared.

Although ownership and legal rights remain with the state, and contractors are required to act under the supervision of and in collaboration with the Forest Service, key functions of post-fire restoration, including its financing, the preparation of studies and the execution of works, can now be undertaken by non-state actors. In this sense, the legislation created a legal framework for the extensive outsourcing of post-disaster environmental governance. It also opened public forest restoration to a model of corporate sponsorship, in which private actors could finance and carry out works in state-owned forests while becoming publicly associated with their restoration or reforestation.

[FIGURE 08: reforestation sponsor image-sponsor] Restoration could therefore generate reputational value for companies and become part of their corporate social responsibility strategies, particularly in the immediate aftermath of extensive and destructive fires, when public attention is most intense. For companies whose wider activities are themselves associated with significant environmental impacts, such as ΔΕΗ and TERNA, which, according to the data presented on this website, rank among the most prominent restoration sponsors, the contradiction is difficult to overlook. Corporations associated with environmental degradation can simultaneously become associated with the restoration of fire-affected landscapes. The scheme therefore not only redistributes post-fire restoration works to private actors, but also reconfigures who gains visibility and political value through the process of environmental restoration.

Alongside the new framework for privately financed forest restoration, legislation adopted after the 2021 fires also changed how publicly funded forest works could be awarded. In October 2021, Article 74 of Law 4843/2021 introduced a special procurement regime for urgent works to control erosion and flooding in areas that had been affected by extensive fires or floods and had been declared under a state of emergency. It allowed contracting authorities to use either direct award below the applicable EU procurement thresholds or negotiation without prior publication in conditions of extreme urgency. In either case, the award procedure was to be completed within ten days of the invitation<sup>10</sup>.

Only two months later, Article 76 of Law 4876/2021 extended the same procedures beyond emergency intervention to forestry works more broadly. They now covered studies and support services, forest clearing, and the maintenance of forest roads and firebreaks, while the provision also removed the ordinary annual aggregate limit on such direct awards<sup>11</sup>. Exceptional procedures introduced for emergency recovery were, in other words, made available for recurring types of works in forest management. For forestry works funded by the state, the change was substantial: the ordinary €60,000 ceiling for direct award was replaced under this special regime by the applicable EU threshold — €5.35 million at the time<sup>12</sup>. This was the procurement framework available when AntiNero programme began to operate at national scale in 2022.

[FIGURE 09: antinero image] AntiNero is a multiannual programme for forest-fire prevention and forest protection across Greece. Introduced in 2022, its current RRF-funded cycle is scheduled to conclude in 2026. Its first works began in May 2022, and the programme was publicly presented by the Ministry of Environment and Energy on 8 July 2022. Its financial framework, however, predates the programme itself. Greece submitted its Recovery and Resilience Plan to the European Commission on 27 April 2021, with Measure 16849 conceived as the National Reforestation Plan and financed through the EU Recovery and Resilience Facility, within the wider policy context of the European Green Deal. The EU Forest Strategy for 2030 followed on 16 July 2021. After the fires of August 2021, the scope of Measure 16849 was enlarged to incorporate AntiNero, bringing reforestation together with fire-prevention and forest-protection works and, later, anti-erosion and flood-protection measures. AntiNero was therefore not created as an implementing instrument of the EU Forest Strategy, although its expanded objectives closely align with the Strategy’s announced emphasis on the restoration of degraded forest ecosystems , resilience and preventive forest management.

[FIGURE 10: types of work graph] Its initial interventions included forest clearings, pruning and thinning, biomass removal, and the maintenance or opening of forest roads and firebreaks. Later phases added fire-prevention planning, mixed fuel breaks, forest nurseries, reforestation studies and works, and post-fire erosion and flood protection. The government has repeatedly promoted AntiNero as the most extensive forest-fire prevention programme ever implemented in Greece. Its remit, however, has become considerably broader, bringing preventive forest management, reforestation and post-fire intervention within the same expanding programme.

The programme’s environmental effects have, however, been strongly contested by forest scientists and environmental organisations, who argue that large-scale interventions have been implemented through relatively uniform technical specifications without sufficient assessment of local ecological conditions, including within Natura 2000 sites. They have raised concerns over intensive understorey removal, disturbance to biodiversity and the timing of works during breeding periods and the summer fire season<sup>13</sup>. These objections have also prompted challenges over whether interventions of this scale should be subject to broader environmental assessment rather than treated as a series of individual forestry works<sup>14</sup>.

[FIGURE 11: fires in Evros] In August 2023, fires that broke out near Alexandroupolis and Dadia merged into a blaze that burned for more than two weeks and consumed over 96,000 hectares, becoming the largest wildfire recorded in the Europe since the 1980s<sup>15</sup>. Twenty people were killed, most of them migrants travelling through the border region. Even as migrants constituted most of the fire’s victims, subsequent analysis of Greek media coverage found migrant blame to be the dominant narrative among reports assigning responsibility for the fires<sup>16</sup>. The post-fire response in Evros drew on arrangements and actors already involved in the recovery of North Evia. The reconstruction effort brought back key figures from that process. Christos Triantopoulos, who had coordinated the government’s recovery measures in North Evia, chaired the new committee, while Stavros Benos, who had led the Diazoma-based reconstruction initiative there, joined as a member. Urgent anti-erosion and flood-protection works were channelled largely through the AntiNero programme and awarded through accelerated procurement procedures. The use of these procedures was itself contested. The Court of Audit initially blocked four Evros contracts on the grounds that their scale and duration could not be justified as an emergency intervention. Those rulings were later overturned, and the contracts were signed in January 2025<sup>17</sup>.

[FIGURE 12: protest against hybrid schemes] The final legislative reform traced here came in May 2024 and extends beyond post fire restoration and prevention programmes to the broader governance of public forests. Law 5106/2024 introduced Hybrid Cooperative Schemes, in which forest workers’ cooperatives collectively hold 50% and private actors the remaining 50%, and created a new route through which these schemes, alongside forest workers’ cooperatives acting independently, can undertake the management and exploitation of designated public forests. The framework ties forest management more directly to the extraction and sale of biomass and to an annual public subsidy, while the award procedure gives greatest weight to the quantity of biomass a bidder commits to remove and the speed with which the works will be completed. Although the two sides of a Hybrid Scheme are formally equal, they enter it from markedly different positions. Private companies must meet substantial financial requirements, while forest workers’ cooperatives may contribute machinery and the labour of their members as capital. The law has been strongly opposed by forest workers’ cooperatives, whose representatives have warned that it strengthens the position of large private companies in the management of public forests<sup>18</sup>. Although the framework is already in force, no Hybrid Cooperative Scheme was identified as operating during this research, so its effects in practice cannot yet be assessed. The law nevertheless already changes the terms under which access to public forest management is organised, creating the conditions for forest workers’ cooperatives to become increasingly dependent on private partners and weakening their capacity to determine the terms of their own work.

Taken together, the changes traced above redraw the terms on which work in Greece’s public forests is commissioned, financed and carried out. What is at stake in these arrangements is not only the amount of public funding provided for fire prevention or post-fire restoration, but also who is authorised to intervene in public forests, whose knowledge and labour shape those interventions, and under what temporal, financial and technical conditions the forestry works are executed. Records of forestry works can therefore be read as a trace of these changing relations. In the context described at the beginning, where fire is experienced as an event and mediated almost as a spectacle, the institutional arrangements made during and after it persist long after the flames and the attention surrounding them have receded, shaping how forests are managed before the next fire season.

[FIGURE 13: fires in Evia] This research therefore asks how the mechanisms introduced since 2021 have changed who receives forestry work in Greece’s public forests, through which procedures, and on what terms. It approaches this question by reading the legislation presented here alongside three sets of records, the designation acts of restoration and reforestation contractors, the contracts of the AntiNero programme, and contracts awarded directly to forest workers’ cooperatives over the same period. Organising and juxtaposing these dispersed records makes visible what successive governmental announcements of funding for forest restoration and reforestation, or fragmented media coverage of post-fire restoration, do not. It begins to trace the different rhythms of procurement, the actors that prevail across different regions and types of work, and allows a comparison of how access to public forests is distributed through publicly funded forestry works.
