The awarding process also changes where the relationship between the state, the forest and the contractor is organised. In the cooperative dataset, around <Num id="dase.forest_eur" /> of the <Num id="dase.total1" /> identified passes through local forest service offices or forest directorates. The work is distributed by a much wider network of public bodies and reaches cooperatives through the territorial structure of the Forest Service. Anti-nero reverses this architecture. All <Num id="antinero.total1" /> is awarded by the Ministry of Environment and Energy through a small number of central operating units, while the local Forest Services principally retain responsibility for supervising and accepting works contracted elsewhere.

The distinction matters because contracting is one of the practices through which knowledge about the forest, the timing of work and relations with forest workers are produced. Moving the awarding of works away from these local relations therefore redistributes not only expenditure but part of the practical capacity to organise forest management. 

The unit of forestry work itself is changing. The cooperative records describe a field made up largely of numerous small and recurring assignments, particularly firewood, logging and silvicultural works, carried out through relations between forest authorities, workers and particular territories. Anti-nero packages intervention differently. Contracts frequently combine studies with execution and extend across several regional units and Forest Services. The enormous difference between the two median contract values therefore matters beyond money. It affects which actors can enter the contract in the first place and transforms work that can be organised in relation to a particular forest and season into larger projects defined through budgets, quantities, technical specifications and deadlines. Forest intervention is made increasingly calculable and contractible, and place-specific processes are converted into discrete projects that can be repeatedly procured.

Fire temporarily disrupts the territorial organisation of cooperative work, while also opening routes that persist beyond the emergency. Evia, Ilia and Evros are among the places where substantial flows of cooperative work coincide with major post-fire interventions. Cooperatives travel beyond their own regions when the scale of restoration demands additional labour. Anti-nero, however, incorporates these post-fire interventions into the same programme that carries out recurring preventive works across the country. This reflects the legislative chronology. Procedures introduced through the urgency of the 2021 fires were extended within months to ordinary forms of forest management, while the programme subsequently broadened from fuel reduction to planning, reforestation and post-fire flood and erosion works. The exceptional intervention therefore does not simply end when the fire does. It becomes repeatable.

Finally, the privately financed projects show that access to public forests is not redistributed only through public money. The restoration and reforestation contractor mechanism has brought 36 companies and organisations into 69 projects, with electricity and energy companies, banks, construction-related firms and other large corporate actors particularly visible. Here the company does not receive a public contract. It finances the intervention itself, while acquiring an authorised role in the restoration of a public forest and the possibility of associating its name with that restoration. The financial scale is much smaller than Anti-nero, but its significance lies elsewhere. It established immediately after the 2021 fires that private capital and expertise could become a route into post-fire forest governance.
